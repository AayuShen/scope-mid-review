=== Slide 1 - Title (0:45) ===

Good morning. We are [TEAM MEMBERS], guided by [GUIDE NAME]. Our project is SCoPe: Trust-Region Latent Planning with JEPA World Models. In one sentence: a robot can plan by imagining what its actions will do, using a learned world model, and SCoPe adds a check so that it only acts on imagined futures the model can actually be trusted on. Two terms first. A world model is a neural network that predicts what happens next if the agent takes an action; think of it as the robot's imagination. And "trust region" here is not the TRPO trust region from policy optimisation; it means the part of the model's internal space where its predictions are reliable. The picture shows the idea: the blue candidate plans stay inside that region and are kept, the grey ones leave it and are rejected.

=== Slide 2 - The problem (1:45) ===

Let me show the problem with one real example from our own runs. The task is Push-T: the blue dot is the agent, and it has to push the grey T-shaped block onto the green T-shaped target. The top row is what actually happened in the simulator. The bottom row is what the world model imagined would happen for exactly the same actions. In imagination the T rotates onto the goal within 25 steps, so the planner chose this plan. In reality the block barely moves, and the episode is never solved. The model was confidently wrong, and nothing in the planner checked. Why does this matter? These planners are now good enough for real robots: a recent model, V-JEPA 2-AC, picked and placed a cup in 80% of trials with no task-specific training, against 15% for a standard robot policy. But prediction errors grow over long horizons, and the obvious fix, penalising uncertainty, actually increased collisions in one study. So our problem statement: planners pick the plan whose imagined future looks best, with no check that the imagination is reliable. And our research question, on the right: can an uncertainty score computed on the imagined states flag unreliable plans well enough that rejecting them improves real success, without throwing away the good plans?

=== Slide 3 - JEPA, DINO-WM and existing work (1:30) ===

Before the related work, what are JEPA models and DINO-WM? JEPA stands for Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, an idea from Yann LeCun's group. Instead of predicting future images pixel by pixel, which is expensive and wastes effort on irrelevant detail like texture and lighting, a JEPA predicts the future in an embedding space: a compact numerical summary of the image. DINO-WM is a world model built this way. It takes a frozen, pretrained image encoder called DINOv2, which turns each 224 by 224 image into 196 small patches, each described by 384 numbers, and it trains a small transformer to predict the next embedding from the current one and an action. It needs no reward, only recorded trajectories and a picture of the goal, and it reaches 90% success on Push-T. That is why it is our base. The table groups the related work. Classic model-based RL, such as PETS, MBPO, MOPO and MOReL, already distrusts uncertain models, but only while training, and on simple state vectors rather than images. Reward-based agents like DreamerV3 need a reward. The JEPA planners plan from images but trust every imagined rollout equally. And recent papers warn that uncertainty can miss the errors that matter, so our scores must be tested. Our difference is the bottom row: one change in the planning loop, the two purple boxes. Score every imagined step, and reject untrusted candidates before choosing a plan.

=== Slide 4 - RL formulation (1:45) ===

Here is the reinforcement-learning formulation. It is an RL problem because a push only takes effect several steps later and success is judged at the end, so actions must be chosen as a sequence, not one at a time. The state is the camera image plus the agent's position, which the encoder turns into the latent state. The action is a 2-D movement target for the agent; we plan in chunks of five simulator steps, five chunks ahead. There is no hand-designed reward: the cost is how far the final imagined embedding is from the goal image's embedding. Real transitions come from the physics simulator, imagined ones from the predictor. An episode is one start and goal pair, and it succeeds when the block is within 20 pixels and 20 degrees of the goal. The algorithm is the cross-entropy method, CEM, inside model-predictive control, MPC. CEM samples 300 candidate action sequences, imagines each one with the world model, keeps the best 30, refits its sampling distribution to them, and repeats 30 times. MPC means we execute the first part of the best plan, observe the real result, and plan again. We chose it over PPO or SAC because we have no reward and only offline data, and over gradient-based planning, which scores only 28% on Push-T. Importantly, CEM keeps an explicit set of candidates, which is exactly what our gate filters. The equation says: minimise the distance to the goal, subject to every imagined step having a trust score below a threshold tau. The data are three simulated tasks, Push-T, Wall and PointMaze, from DINO-WM's public release.

=== Slide 5 - System architecture (1:45) ===

This is the system architecture; follow the blue numbers. The middle layer is the agent at decision time: the frozen world model, the CEM-MPC planner, and our new trust module. One: the simulator sends the next image to the planner. Two: the planner asks the world model to encode it and to imagine 300 candidate futures. Three: those imagined states go to the trust module, which returns which candidates are accepted. Four: the planner executes the chosen actions in the simulator. Five: the true state goes to a success checker that is used only for evaluation; the agent never receives a reward. The top layer is offline. The world model checkpoints come from the DINO-WM authors; we do not retrain them. Six, in purple: we log the imagined states next to what really happened, which gives us an error-labelled dataset. Seven: we use that dataset to calibrate the trust scores and the threshold, then load them into the trust module. That offline loop is where SCoPe learns. The tags show status: grey is reused code, green is built by us and working, amber means only the interface exists, and purple dashed is planned.

=== Slide 6 - Evidence so far (1:45) ===

What have we shown so far? First, we reproduced DINO-WM on our own 8 GB laptop GPU. On the left, each grey dot is one seed of 50 episodes, blue is our average, orange the paper: 87% after one plan against 86%, and 90% with replanning, matching the paper. Second, on the right, a first pilot of our own idea. On 80 Push-T episodes over three seeds we ran 192 of CEM's candidate plans each in the simulator and compared what the model imagined with what really happened: 76,800 imagined steps. We marked the 10% with the largest real error and asked whether a trust score ranks exactly those highest. That is the AUROC; 0.5 is a coin flip, and no score is trained yet. MC-dropout, which runs the predictor four times with dropout on and measures how much the runs disagree, scores 0.86, between 0.84 and 0.88 on every seed, and its whole interval is above the trivial baseline, how far the plan moves, at 0.79. The other scores are weaker, and the planner's own cost is at chance. So the first half of our hypothesis holds in this pilot. The honest caveat is the second tile: replaying the gate offline barely changed the chosen plan's success, 71% without a gate and 73% even with an oracle gate that knows the true error, so any real gain must show up in the closed loop. Wall, over three seeds, reaches 95% after three rounds against the paper's 96%, and PointMaze reaches 100%.

=== Slide 7 - Progress and next steps (1:15) ===

Where we stand and what comes next. Our novel components are an error-labelled dataset of imagined versus real states and a calibration test of trust scores; both now exist as a pilot, which is the amber tag. The gated planner with its fallback ladder is still planned, and the interfaces and the speed recipe are built. Of ten work packages, four are done, four partial and two not started. The roadmap: weeks one to three for the logger and the dataset, weeks four to six for the offline test of hypothesis H1, with a go or no-go decision at the end of week six: we only build the closed loop if a score beats both random and the trivial baseline with non-overlapping intervals. The pilot already passes that bar for MC-dropout, on three seeds. The box at the bottom left says what we still have to do before trusting it: more sampling contexts and calibration, and then the real test, H4: does gating raise success compared with plain planning, a random gate and an oracle gate? Even a negative answer is a useful result. Thank you; we are happy to take questions.
